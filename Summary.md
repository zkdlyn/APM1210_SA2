## APM1210 Summative Assessment

### Summary of Findings

In the Philippines, celebrities have long influenced politics by endorsing candidates and using their mass appeal to elevate visibility and sway voters. Over time, these stars began running for office themselves, turning their screen fame into political power. In 1958, matinee idol Rogelio dela Rosa became the first film celebrity to win a Senate seat. Four decades later, in 1998, action star Joseph Estrada rose to presidency. Popular media personalities continue occupying seats today, with over 70 running candidates in the recent 2025 elections.

The sampled dataset for this study comprises 30 actors—18 classified as non-politicians and 12 as politicians. It includes columns for career length, total acting credits, number of immediate family members in politics, highest educational attainment, and number of acting awards won from notable bodies.

Actors are found to likely have at least one family connection in politics, blurring the lines between politics and showbiz in the Philippines. This phenomenon highlights name recall as a prized currency in electoral success. Their household names, backed by established campaign machinery and loyal voter networks—exemplified by the Ejercito-Estrada and Revilla clans—give them a clear edge when running for public office.

The permutation test found no significant difference in the average number of award wins between actors who entered politics and those who did not. This suggests that variations in total award wins alone do not reliably predict an actor’s electoral success. While awards undoubtedly boost public recognition and reflect professional caliber, individual career priorities may vary widely—whether toward politics or continued acting gigs. To uncover stronger predictors of political success among actors, future studies should incorporate broader measures of fame and other contextual factors.

Among the predictors used in the predictive model, family emerged as a powerful and significant factor in an actor's political career prospects. Supporting the bootsrap estimation findings, actors who have acquired on-screen fame paired wih a politically dominant surname can almost guarantee themselves a government seat. Career length follows closely as a positive predictor, suggesting that longer public exposure and relevancy through film and television may strengthen an actor's campaign. In contrast, award wins and total number of acting credits are slightly negatively associated with being actor-politicans. Fewer roles or awards for actor-politicians may likely be a consequence of their divided priotities. Meanwhile, actors who stay solely in showbiz can afford to get more acting jobs and earn more accolades.

While accolades and public recognition may not distinguish those who enter politics from those who don't, <ins>**education**</ins> appears as another decisive pattern based from the conducted MCMC Bayesian Inference. Celebrities who pivot into political life appear to engage in a notable form of **image reconstruction**, one that is centered distantly from their entertainment credentials. The data reveal that individuals who have held elected office or intend to do so are significantly more likely to pursue higher levels of formal education, most especially postgraduate degrees. In a space where credibility is constantly under scrutiny, educational attainment functions as a form of <ins>**reputational capital**</ins>. These public figures are making deliberate investments in public trust. Academic credentials then become their <ins>**strategic assets**</ins>, bridging the perceived gap between celebrity and public service in the large societal eye. Indeed, this fusion of fame and academic legitimacy has become a compelling formula for political viability.

Taken together with the education patterns, it is clear that more than just the formal credentials, the overall structure of a celebrity's career is quite paramount to their political success. The bivariate density plots reveal a concentration of individuals with lengthy, stable careers and a moderate-to-high number of acting credits. These aren't necessarily the biggest stars or most decorated actors, but their careers suggest something equally significant. It is that they have the special kind of earned familiarity and soft, quiet cultural impact. Such public figures tend to occupy a middle space in public consciousness: <ins>**not idolized, but recognizable**</ins>. While not exactly always the case (e.g., Vilma Santos), too much of the wrong kind of fame could definitely be detrimental for political endeavors as shown by the cases of Willie Revillame and many others. That positioning can be a potent weapon in political campaigns, where name recognition is crucial but reckless overexposure or controversy can be liabilities. A sustained career, especially one that avoids scandals, can serve as a kind of <ins>**soft credential**</ins> in their favor. Consequently, this signifies as an informal proof of their resilience, adaptability, and consistent public appeal- at least to the unassuming mind. In this aspect, the density peak (mode) encapsulates a specific kind of public trajectory that lends itself well to <ins>**political rebranding**</ins>. These individuals have, over time, built a foundation of visibility and perceived authenticity that can be strategically re-leveraged in political life. Their careers offer enough narrative flexibility to pivot into roles of public leadership, while their long-term media presence anchors them as familiar faces.

The findings point to a clear pattern among Filipino celebrities who have transitioned into politics: most successful entrants are just at the sweet spot of not being the most decorated or critically acclaimed, yet still with sustained public visibility and strategic personal positioning. While no significant difference was found in the number of prestigious awards won between celebrities who entered politics and those who did not, the subsequent analyses revealed that those who pursued political office are more likely to have obtained higher levels of formal education, including postgraduate degrees. This suggests that educational attainment may function as a compensatory mechanism in an effort to bolster credibility and project competence. As celebrity status alone may be viewed with skepticism, these public figures may be inclined to obtain these academic supplements despite previous studies showing educational attainment not being a significant factor at all. Additionally, the cluster of individuals characterized by moderate-to-high acting credits and long career spans likely reflects a segment of the entertainment industry that has built familiarity and trust over time. These figures are well-positioned to reframe their public identity and mobilize existing social capital for electoral gain. Populist politics rewards those who can turn fame into familiarity, and familiarity into trust. Visbility becomes recognition, education becomes legitimacy, and legacy becomes the story they sell.

### Recommendations

Future studies should consider expanding the dataset beyond the initial 124 observations to strengthen generalizability and statistical power. Since existing data are so limited, sampling to an even smaller may not be the best course of action for this research endeavor. Although purposive sampling is justified due to the limited number of actor-politicians, incorporating additional comparable figures, such as musicians or television personalities, may help clarify whether observed patterns are specific to actors or extend across broader entertainment sectors. Moreover, while the current dataset captures key structural indicators such as career longevity, award recognition, education, and political lineage, future research could benefit from integrating qualitative dimensions like public sentiment, social media engagement, campaign narratives, or political alignments to contextualize the transition from entertainment to politics more fully. It is also recommended that subsequent analyses consider temporal changes, as political opportunities and media environments may have shifted over the 20-year period examined (2005–2025). Narrowing the time range could also be better to reflect contemporary voter trends, unless researchers also want to map out the steady decline of celebrity politics in recent times. Finally, expanding the educational and family background variables to include quality or prestige (e.g., university ranking, political office held by family members) may offer a more holistic understanding of how symbolic capital is leveraged in the political field.
